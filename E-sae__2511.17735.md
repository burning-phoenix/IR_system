# Towards Open-Ended Visual Scientific Discovery with Sparse Autoencoders

Jacob Beattie  Email: *beattie.74@osu.edu Samuel Stevens  Affiliation: The Ohio State University  Neil Rosser  Affiliation: The University of Miami  Yu Su  Affiliation: The Ohio State University  Tanya Berger-Wolf  Affiliation: The Ohio State University 

###### Abstract

Foundation models in several scientific domains, including visual domains, learn representations that capture complex semantics from their respective fields. Despite this, most existing applications focus on pre-specified concepts or targets, excelling at confirmation but not open-ended discovery of unknown patterns. We study whether sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with heuristic feature ranking can provide a practical methodology for surfacing candidate visual features from vision foundation models for scientific discovery without concept-specific supervision. We evaluate this approach in three stages: (1) general-purpose concept rediscovery, (2) domain-specific concept rediscovery, and (3) question-driven feature ranking. On ADE20K, SAEs outperform baseline methods ( $k$ -means, PCA, SemiNMF) across most downstream metrics for concept rediscovery. On FishVista, the same approach recovers fine-grained fish anatomical features without concept supervision. Finally, on Heliconius butterflies, a heuristic based on the Mann-Whitney $U$ statistic consistently surfaces features corresponding to real diagnostic keys between mimic subspecies. Together, these results show that SAEs with heuristic ranking can efficiently learn and surface semantic structure in vision foundation model representations. We view this as a methodological prerequisite for open-ended visual scientific discovery: the present experiments validate feature surfacing and ranking through rediscovery, while prospective discovery of previously unknown scientific phenomena remains future work.

## 1 Introduction

Scientific data now span hundreds of petabytes across domains, far outpacing what any lab can annotate or manually inspect.11 1 For example, the NIH Sequence Read Archive exposes roughly $47$ PB of public sequencing data, the European Nucleotide Archive provides roughly $63$ PB of immediately downloadable data, NASA Earthdata (EOSDIS) hosts more than $120$ PB of open Earth science data, and a single reanalysis, ERA5, is on the order of $5$ PB. Large neural networks trained on these datasets cover proteins (Abramson et al., 2024), weather (Lam et al., 2023; Price et al., 2025), genomics (Cui et al., 2024; Hayes et al., 2025), geospatial (Reed et al., 2023), and ecology (Gu et al., 2025). These models achieve strong predictive performance across a variety of tasks by learning compressed representations of the underlying data distribution’s structure.

However, foundation models are currently limited to _confirmatory_ applications where the target concepts are pre-specified (see Figure 2). Foundation models are used as frozen feature extractors or fine-tuned for specific predictions (species classification (Van Horn et al., 2021), cellular responses to perturbations (Roohani et al., 2025), 3-D protein structure (Haas et al., 2018; Pereira et al., 2021)). Because of this, the representations produced by foundation models are high-dimensional embeddings optimized for task performance. While these representations enable accurate predictions, they do not directly provide reusable scientific measurements or expose the structure the model learned for systematic investigation.

#### Discovery vs. Confirmation

Scientific discovery often involves establishing names and descriptions of specific, previously unexplained phenomena (Kuhn and Hacking, 1970; Poincaré, 2003). A morphological feature that distinguishes subspecies but is not formally cataloged, an unexplored correlation between climate variables, or a sequence motif in an understudied protein family: these cannot be found by methods that require specifying target concepts in advance. Here, we focus on “open-ended feature discovery”: surfacing candidate visual features that are relevant to a specific scientific question, without specifying relevant concepts beforehand (Hughes et al., 2024). Because genuinely novel scientific findings cannot be evaluated against ground truth, we evaluate this capability through controlled rediscovery of known concepts and traits.

Interpretability methods offer a route to systematically surface the semantic structure learned by foundation models. While many existing interpretability methods excel at confirmation (Alain and Bengio, 2016; Kim et al., 2018; Selvaraju et al., 2017; Sundararajan et al., 2017; Simonyan et al., 2013), they cannot perform open-ended exploration to generate hypotheses (see Figure 2). In particular, methods for such open-ended discovery from vision foundation model representations remain underdeveloped and untested.

#### Our approach.

We propose sparse autoencoders (SAEs) with heuristic ranking as a step towards open-ended visual feature discovery. SAEs learn an overcomplete dictionary that reconstructs model activations through sparse linear combinations, with sparsity encouraging individual features to capture semantically coherent patterns (Bricken et al., 2023; Cunningham et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024; Gao et al., 2025). Unlike supervised methods, SAEs require no labels during training. Each learned feature provides a per-example activation score together with its decoding direction and exemplar evidence, enabling systematic investigation of what patterns the model represents. We then propose question-driven heuristics to rank and quickly surface relevant and possibly novel visual features. In short, we treat SAEs with heuristic feature ranking as a potential tool for open-ended visual feature discovery from foundation models and seek to rigorously evaluate that tool in controlled settings (Peng et al., 2025).

On ADE20K, we find that SAEs outperform baseline methods ( $k$ -means clustering, PCA, and SemiNMF) in concept alignment (Sections 4.1 and 4.1), rediscovering known segmentation concepts without supervision. On FishVista, we find that SAEs learn fine-grained anatomical features, providing measurable quantities that can be evaluated against biological annotations. Finally, we find that a modified AUC-ROC score highly ranks known diagnostic traits across several mimic subspecies pairs, relying only on species labels (Section 4.3). Our results indicate that SAEs with heuristic ranking can learn and effectively surface semantic structure from vision foundation model representations. We view this work as a prerequisite step to visual scientific discovery: establishing a methodology that can systematically recover and rank known structure from images, making search for truly novel scientific phenomena feasible.

## 2 Related Work

#### Foundation Models.

Domain-specific foundation models capture rich structure and achieve state-of-the-art performance on their focal tasks, as shown in proteins (Abramson et al., 2024; Hayes et al., 2025), ecology (Stevens et al., 2024), weather (Lam et al., 2023; Price et al., 2025), and single-cell genomics (Cui et al., 2024). In both general and scientific domains, large-scale training leads to emergent abilities beyond the pre-training objective (Wei et al., 2022; Gu et al., 2025). Theory and evidence suggest that large pretrained models internalize meaningful structure (Ha and Schmidhuber, 2018; Caron et al., 2021; LeCun, 2022; Huh et al., 2024). Despite this, most scientific models are benchmarked as task-specific predictors rather than sources of novel concepts.

#### Interpretability Methods.

A large body of work explains predictions without exposing a global factorization of representations. Saliency and attribution methods (e.g., gradients, Integrated Gradients, Grad-CAM) highlight input regions for _individual_ decisions and have known validity pitfalls (Simonyan et al., 2013; Sundararajan et al., 2017; Selvaraju et al., 2017; Adebayo et al., 2018). Linear probes test separability of _pre-specified_ targets (Alain and Bengio, 2016; Hewitt and Liang, 2019); concept methods such as TCAV/ACE require exemplar sets and so test investigator-defined ideas (Kim et al., 2018; Ghorbani et al., 2019). Prototype models (ProtoPNet, ProtoTree, ProtoPFormer) provide case-based, class-tied explanations but are trained end-to-end for specific tasks (Chen et al., 2019; Nauta et al., 2021; Xue et al., 2022). Activation maximization and “feature visualization” (DeepDream-style) optimize inputs for chosen units to aid intuition, but produce synthetic, non-natural images (Mahendran and Vedaldi, 2016; Nguyen et al., 2016; Olah et al., 2017). These approaches excel at explaining predictions or testing for known concepts (confirmation) but cannot be used to discover patterns without prior specification (Figure 2).

#### Sparse Autoencoders & Decomposition.

Classical dictionary learning and sparse coding demonstrated that sparse, parts-based factors can emerge from natural data (Olshausen and Field, 1996; Aharon et al., 2006; Lee and Seung, 1999). Recent work applies sparse autoencoders (SAEs) to model activations, showing that sparse decompositions can partially “unmix” superposed features and yield monosemantic directions (Elhage et al., 2022; Bricken et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2025; Joseph et al., 2025) in language and vision models. SAEs address the superposition hypothesis (Elhage et al., 2022) by assigning features to directions in activation space rather than individual neurons. (Gao et al., 2025) demonstrate that $k$ -sparse autoencoders scale effectively and improve reconstruction–sparsity tradeoffs. Furthermore, concurrent work argues for using SAEs for discovering unknown concepts rather than acting on known ones (Peng et al., 2025) and applies dictionary learning to microscopy foundation models for biological concept extraction (Donhauser et al., 2024). Beyond SAEs, concept factorization methods such as CRAFT, ACE, and related work use NMF-like decompositions to extract human-interpretable concepts and quantify their importance (Fel et al., 2023b; Fel et al., 2023a; Ghorbani et al., 2019). Finally, recent analyses highlight stability and identifiability limits of SAE dictionaries across runs (Leask et al., 2025), motivating careful evaluation protocols.

#### Discovery.

Closest in intent to our goal are (i) work advocating SAEs for uncovering _unknown_ concepts rather than acting on _known_ ones (Peng et al., 2025), (ii) dictionary-learning approaches that extract biological concepts from microscopy or epigenetics foundation models (Donhauser et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2026), and (iii) automated concept discovery pipelines that discover concepts and then name or validate them with limited supervision (Rao et al., 2024). We differ by offering a comprehensive analysis of SAEs for visual feature discovery, validating both on a standard semantic benchmark and an ecology case study and demonstrating how to use heuristics to enable discovery in practice. We also differ from unsupervised dense segmentation methods (LOST, TokenCut, STEGO, MaskCut) by returning global features with per-patch activations rather than per-image masks or clusters (Siméoni et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2023b; Hamilton et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2023a).

## 3 Methodology

#### Foundation Model.

We use the publicly available DINOv3 ViT-L/16 checkpoint (Siméoni et al., 2025), additionally using Perception Encoder ViT-L/14 336 (Bolya et al., 2025) in ablations (Section 4.1). Images are resized with an aspect-ratio-aware transform that enforces exactly $256$ patches per image (a $h\times w$ patch grid with $hw=256$ chosen to minimize aspect-ratio distortion), instead of forcing square $256\times 256$ inputs. We then extract patch-token activations ([CLS] and [REG] discarded).

#### Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs).

Our dictionary learner is a ReLU SAE (Bricken et al., 2023; Templeton et al., 2024); given a $d$ -dimensional activation vector $\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ from an intermediate layer $l$ of a transformer, an SAE maps $\mathbf{x}$ to a sparse representation $f(\mathbf{x})$ (Equations 1 and 2) and reconstructs the original input (Equation 3):

|  $\displaystyle\mathbf{h}$  |  $\displaystyle=W_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}-b_{\text{dec}})+b_{\text{enc}}$  |  | (1)  
---|---|---|---|---  
|  $\displaystyle f(\mathbf{x})$  |  $\displaystyle=\text{ReLU}(\mathbf{h})$  |  | (2)  
|  $\displaystyle\mathbf{\hat{x}}$  |  $\displaystyle=W_{\text{dec}}f(\mathbf{x})+b_{\text{dec}}$  |  | (3)  
  
where $W_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ , $b_{\text{enc}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$ , $W_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n}$ and $b_{\text{dec}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}$ . The training objective minimizes reconstruction error while encouraging sparsity:

|  $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{\hat{x}}||_{2}^{2}+\lambda\mathcal{S}(f(\mathbf{x}))$$  |  | (4)  
---|---|---|---  
  
where $\lambda$ controls the sparsity penalty and $\mathcal{S}$ measures sparsity (L1 norm for training, L0 for model selection).

We train $16\,384$ -latent SAEs for 100M patches from a single ViT layer and sweep a logarithmic grid of sparsity coefficients $\lambda$ and learning rates. We summarize each trained model by its reconstruction quality (normalized MSE) and sparsity (L0). Normalized MSE (NMSE) is MSE normalized by a mean baseline MSE:

|  $$\text{NMSE}=\frac{\sum_{i}||\mathbf{x_{i}}-\mathbf{\hat{x}_{i}}||_{2}^{2}}{\sum_{i}||\mathbf{x_{i}}-\mathbf{\bar{x}}||_{2}^{2}}$$  |  | (5)  
---|---|---|---  
  
where $\mathbf{\hat{x}_{i}}$ is the predicted reconstruction and $\mathbf{\bar{x}}$ is the mean of all $\mathbf{x_{i}}$ .

#### SAE variants.

We also use TopK (Gao et al., 2025) and Matryoshka SAEs (Bussmann et al., 2025) and investigate their impact on concept rediscovery.

Matryoshka SAEs (Bussmann et al., 2025) address feature splitting (Bricken et al., 2023; Chanin et al., 2024; Leask et al., 2025) by training multiple nested dictionaries simultaneously. Each training step samples random prefixes of SAE latents and reconstructs the input using each prefix. We sample ten prefixes $\mathcal{M}={m_{1},m_{2},\ldots,m_{10}}$ with $m_{i}\in\{1,...,16384\}$ ; the sum of prefix reconstruction losses replaces MSE in the objective:

|  $$\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\sum_{m\in\mathcal{M}}||\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{0:m}||^{2}_{2}+\lambda\mathcal{S}(f(\mathbf{x}))$$  |  | (6)  
---|---|---|---  
  
where $\mathbf{\hat{x}}_{0:m}$ is the reconstruction using only the first $m$ latents. This encourages early latents to capture more general features, implicitly ordering them by decreasing reconstruction importance (analogous to PCA). At inference, prefixes are not sampled, making Matryoshka SAEs function identically to vanilla SAEs.

TopK SAEs (Gao et al., 2025) replace the ReLU activation and sparsity objective $\mathcal{S}$ with a TopK selection over latents, retaining only the $k$ largest activations per input and zeroing the rest. This enforces a fixed sparsity level and improves feature selectivity relative to ReLU at comparable reconstruction error.

#### Ranking Heuristic.

When applying SAEs for visual feature discovery in Heliconius butterflies, we focus on finding diagnostic traits between two species. For each SAE feature, we first reduce its activation map for a given image to a single scalar by taking the maximum activation value across all spatial locations (patches). We then score each SAE feature for species separation using a rank-based separability statistic built from the Mann-Whitney U-statistic (Mann and Whitney, 1947; Mason and Graham, 2002), without training a classifier. We call this metric selectivity: a value of 1 denotes perfect separation between subspecies, and 0 denotes chance-level separation.

|  $\displaystyle\text{sel.}=|\frac{U}{n_{1}n_{2}}-0.5|*2$  |  | (7)  
---|---|---|---  
  
where $U$ is the Mann-Whitney U-statistic calculated using the reduced SAE feature values and $n_{1},n_{2}$ are the counts for each respective species. See Appendix C for a full definition.

## 4 Experiments

We evaluate the proposed method for open-ended visual discovery quality through _controlled rediscovery_ of ground-truth concepts withheld from SAE training. Strong alignment on known concepts indicates that the same procedure is a promising method for surfacing meaningful structure where ground truth is unavailable.

Discovery is aided by monosemantic features: each latent should align with a single coherent concept. We measure concept alignment with 1-D logistic probes, the standard evaluation in the SAE literature (Gao et al., 2025). We report (1) probe $R$ : loss $\mathcal{L}$ normalized by a bias-only baseline $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}$ : $1-(\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{L}_{\pi})$ , (2) mean Average Precision (mAP) across all classes, (3) Purity@ $k$ : precision of the top- $k$ activated patches for each feature, and (4) Coverage@ $\tau$ : the fraction of classes whose best-aligned feature achieves AP $\geq\tau$ . We select the SAE with the best probe loss on the training split and report validation metrics. For exact definition of all metrics, see Appendix C.

### 4.1 General semantic rediscovery

We use ADE20K Zhou et al. (2017) as a controlled testbed for rediscovery: can label-free SAE features align with segmentation classes? SAEs are trained on ImageNet-1K activations only; ADE20K labels enter solely at evaluation, isolating extraction from supervision. Concretely, we extract activations on ImageNet-1K (train/val) and ADE20K (train/val) from the final layer of DINOv3 ViT-L/16.

#### Baseline Methods.

We compare SAEs to standard unsupervised baselines: $k$ -means, principal component analysis (PCA), and semi-nonnegative matrix factorization (Ding et al., 2008, SemiNMF;). We fit all baselines on ImageNet-1K training activations. For $k$ -means, we use $k$ = $16\,384$ clusters and use the nearest cluster as the predicted reconstruction with a fixed L0= $1$ . We fit PCA with $n$ components $n\in\{1,4,16,64,256,1024\}$ . For SemiNMF, we use a mini-batch semi-NMF estimator with nonnegative codes and an unconstrained dictionary; codes are inferred by multiplicative updates with cached positive/negative Gram terms, while the dictionary is updated from accumulated $Z^{\top}Z$ and $Z^{\top}A$ statistics with a ridge term. For all methods, we report normalized reconstruction MSE on ImageNet-1K (validation split). See Appendix G for more details.

#### Discussion.

$k$ -means and PCA are competitive on reconstruction–sparsity trade-offs, yet underperform SAEs on downstream concept-alignment metrics such as mAP, Purity@ $k$ , and Coverage@ $\tau$ . This reinforces that normalized MSE and L0 measure how well activations are compressed, not how well individual latents track semantic concepts. ### 4.3 Question-driven feature ranking

Previously, we established that SAEs are a strong decomposition method for learning visual features. Heliconius butterflies are an often-studied example of mimicry and evolution (McMillan et al., 2020); understanding subspecies wing patterns and traits is of great interest to researchers (Hines et al., 2011; Jiggins, 2008; McMillan et al., 2020). Natural selection has driven striking convergence in the appearance of different Heliconius butterfly species, such that different species can appear almost identical (Reed et al., 2011). Nonetheless, decades of work by biologists have revealed certain key traits, such as subtle differences in color pattern, that can be used to distinguish mimics (Müller, 1879; DeVries, 1987; Jiggins, 2008; Lawrence et al., 2026). We propose using SAEs with our selectivity heuristic (Section 3) to identify visual features of Heliconius wings that may separate mimic subspecies. To test the efficacy of using this approach in practice, we test whether it can efficiently rediscover known diagnostic traits between Heliconius erato and Heliconius melpomene subspecies. We train Matryoshka TopK SAEs on DINOv3 layer 22 and 24 patch activations of 24.8K Heliconius images (Lawrence et al., 2024) and rank features for selectivity between three mimic pairs. We then compare the highest selectivity score across all values of $k$ in the Pareto-optimal SAEs.

First, we find that layer 22 consistently provides higher selectivity scores, indicating that the last layer may not be optimal for all cases. Then, a co-author with domain expertise in Heliconius wing-patterns manually annotated the 5 highest-ranked features from layer 22 pareto-optimal SAEs for each mimic pair, determining whether each corresponded to a known diagnostic trait. We find that in all 3 mimic pairs, the Matryoshka TopK SAE features with the highest selectivity values correspond to known diagnostic traits (Figure 6, Appendix I) (Cast, 2025; Lawrence et al., 2026). Additionally, for each mimic pair, 60-100% of the five highest-ranked features by selectivity corresponded to known diagnostic traits. We provide images and annotations of the five highest-ranked features by selectivity for each mimic pair in Appendix I. Exact SAE training parameters are provided in Appendix E.

#### Discussion.

The consistently high rank of features corresponding with expert-validated diagnostic traits in Heliconius mimic pairs suggests that using well-designed heuristics enables quick identification of scientifically interesting features, without the need for fine-grained concept labels. ## 5 Conclusion

In the settings we studied, we demonstrated that sparse autoencoders can systematically recover semantic structure from vision foundation model representations without concept supervision, using controlled rediscovery on both general-domain visual concepts and a scientific case study. On ADE20K, SAEs consistently rediscovered semantic units from DINOv3 activations trained only on ImageNet-1K, outperforming decomposition baselines ( $k$ -means, PCA & SemiNMF) on almost all concept alignment metrics. On ecological images, the same procedure surfaced anatomical structures without using segmentation or part labels during extraction. Finally, when used with simple ranking heuristics, features corresponding with known diagnostic traits were easily identified. Our results provide evidence that SAEs are a practical method for extracting interpretable features from vision foundation models without needing supervision. With this, the quantitative nature of SAE features shifts the search for interesting visual phenomena to a heuristic design problem, allowing researchers to better leverage foundation model representations and more quickly create meaningful hypotheses. Importantly, our contributions should be understood as validating SAEs as an instrument for open-ended visual discovery, not as delivering novel biological or physical discoveries themselves. In the future, we aim to use this approach in collaboration with domain experts to extract truly novel scientific insights from vision foundation models.

## Appendix A Limitations

While the proposed methodology effectively learns and identifies meaningful, relevant visual features for discovery, advances are still needed to better enable discovery. First, our evaluation relies on ground-truth annotations to measure concept alignment, which limits our ability to assess discovery in truly unannotated domains. Second, previous work has demonstrated issues with SAE features, such as instability (Paulo and Belrose, 2025; Papadimitriou et al., 2025; Leask et al., 2025) and splitting or absorption (Chanin et al., 2024). With this, vision features can be difficult to interpret, and SAE features may not perfectly align with a human interpretation of them. As such, it may be necessary to include additional mechanisms for interpretability (Shaham et al., 2024; He et al., 2026) or leverage multimodal models. This approach also only provides possibly interesting features, and does not perform fully-automatic scientific discovery. However, we argue that visual scientific discovery necessarily requires expert validation and reasoning. Finally, while strong performance on two domains suggests the method will generalize, we leave both systematic evaluation of novel discoveries and applying SAEs to non-vision foundation models to future work.

## Appendix C Metric Definitions

We propose selectivity as a heuristic for identifying features corresponding to known diagnostic traits in Heliconius butterflies. We use a variety of metrics to evaluate SAE probe quality. We include scatter plots of our different metrics (Probe $R$ , mAP, Purity@ $k$ and Coverage@ $\tau$ ) in Figure C1.

#### Selectivity

Let $x_{1},...,x_{n_{1}}$ and $y_{1},...,y_{n_{2}}$ be SAE feature values (max per image) corresponding to 2 classes. Then, let $T$ be the sum of the ranks of values $y_{1},...,y_{n_{2}}$ in the ordered sequence of $x$ ’s and $y$ ’s. We then have:

|  $\displaystyle U=n_{1}n_{2}+\frac{n_{2}(n_{2}+1)}{2}-T$  |  | (8)  
---|---|---|---  
  
where $U$ is the Mann-Whitney $U$ -statistic (Mann and Whitney, 1947). Therefore, in full we define selectivity as:

|  $\displaystyle\text{sel.}=|\frac{n_{1}n_{2}+\frac{n_{2}(n_{2}+1)}{2}-T}{n_{1}n_{2}}-0.5|*2$  |  | (9)  
---|---|---|---  
  
#### Probe $R$ .

For each ground truth concept, we fit a binary logistic on each latent $i$ ’s activation $z_{i}$ and select the best latent per class:

|  $$\min_{i,w,b}\mathbb{E}\big[-y\log\sigma(wz_{i}+b)-(1-y)\log(1-\sigma(wz_{i}+b))\big]$$  |  | (10)  
---|---|---|---  
  
Because the probe is axis-aligned by design, it favors representations whose individual axes carry semantic meaning. This is not a limitation of the evaluation but a requirement of the use case: features that cannot be understood one at a time cannot be browsed for discovery.

We record the binary cross entropy loss (see Equation 10) for the trained probe ( $\mathcal{L}$ ) and the loss for a bias-only probe ( $\mathcal{L}_{\pi}$ ). We normalize the trained loss by the bias-only loss: $1-(\mathcal{L}/\mathcal{L}_{\pi})$ .

#### mAP.

For each class, we treat its patches as positives and all others as negatives, rank all validation patches by the corresponding probe score, and compute average precision as the area under the precision–recall curve. We report mean average precision (mAP) as the macro-average of per-class AP values, assigning AP= $0$ to classes without positive examples.

#### Purity@ $k$ .

For each SAE latent $i$ , we take the $k$ image patches that maximally activate latent $i$ and measure how many of the $k$ patches have the same patch label, then normalize by $k$ to get a score between $0$ and $1$ . We set $k$ = $16$ in our work.

#### Coverage@ $\tau$ .

We measure the fraction of classes ( $151$ for ADE20K, $10$ for FishVista) that have an SAE latent with a minimum average precision of $\tau$ . We set $\tau$ = $0.3$ in our work.

## Appendix F Ablation Results

We report all metrics for the best SAE for each ViT size and layer in Table H3.

#### Model size.

Model scale consistently correlates with downstream performance (Kaplan et al., 2020; Hoffmann et al., 2022). Prior work proposes scaling laws for SAE reconstruction based on both SAE and foundation model size (Gao et al., 2025) but does not investigate downstream performance. We assess foundation model size effects by training SAEs on patch activations from DINOv3 ViT-S/16, ViT-B/16, and ViT-L/16 checkpoints. The models vary in both total parameter count ( $22$ M, $86$ M and $303$ M, respectively) and the embedding dimension $d$ ( $384$ , $768$ , and $1024$ , respectively). For each size, we keep the SAE width fixed at $16\,384$ , train on ImageNet-1K $100$ M patch activations and probe the SAEs for ADE20K features.

In Figure F3 (left) we see that larger models are in fact harder to reconstruct (note that the $y$ -axis is raw rather than normalized MSE), consistent with prior work in scaling SAEs for language models (Gao et al., 2025). Despite more challenging dictionary learning, we see in Appendix H that SAEs applied to larger models both recover more concepts (coverage) and are more precise (purity). All three sizes of the DINOv3 ViTs are distilled from a single, larger teacher model (Siméoni et al., 2025); we conjecture then that all three checkpoints have learned the same set of concepts. These results indicate that, at least for vision, strong foundation models in combination with SAEs can rediscover semantic concepts, which we view as a prerequisite for scientific discovery.

A potential confounder is total training FLOPs: prior work shows that additional SAE training compute (total FLOPs) leads to stronger SAEs as measured by the reconstruction–sparsity tradeoff (Gao et al., 2025). Our SAEs trained on larger embedding dimensions $d$ use more compute during training; this could explain the stronger results for larger models. However, our SAEs trained on larger models have worse reconstruction–sparsity but better downstream concept alignment metrics.

#### Layer depth.

Representations evolve across network depth: later layers in CNNs and ViTs tend to encode higher-level, more linearly separable semantics, which often improves transfer and dense alignment (Yosinski et al., 2014; Azizpour et al., 2015; Caron et al., 2021). However, recent results show that the final layer is not always optimal (Bolya et al., 2025). Intermediate layers can yield less entangled, more spatially localized features depending on the downstream task. We therefore evaluate six layers in the second half of the transformer for the three evaluated checkpoints: for the $24$ -layer ViT-L/16 model, we use layers $14$ , $16$ , $18$ , $20$ , $22$ , and $24$ and for the $12$ -layer ViT-S/16 and ViT-B/16 we use the last $6$ layers $7$ , $8$ , $9$ , $10$ , $11$ , and $12$ .

In Figure F3, we find that later layers are harder to reconstruct (note that the $y$ -axis is raw rather than normalized MSE). We also evaluate downstream metrics (mAP) for the best SAE at each layer for all three ViT checkpoints in Figure F4. Despite more challenging reconstruction, we find that later layers lead to better recovery for all three checkpoints. Prior work in SAEs for language models finds that later layers are harder to reconstruct except for the final two layers (Gao et al., 2025). Language models use a linear embedding layer for next-token-prediction; the final layers must optimize for linear separability. In contrast, DINOv3 uses multiple, loss-specific multilayer perceptrons with additional non-linearities for pre-training (Siméoni et al., 2025); we conjecture this architectural difference leads to the difference in our results.
